# 05 · Slide design: hierarchy, focus, accessibility

These are working heuristics adapted from UX research and presentation literature. They are not strict laws. A stage is not a website, so don't copy every interface rule onto slides.

## About "Death by PowerPoint"
The phrase describes a talk that wears the audience out with overloaded, badly organized or irrelevant slides. Two well-known sources use it:

- **Alexei Kapterev, *Death by PowerPoint*.** His slide deck about slide decks grew into the book *Presentation Secrets* (Wiley, 2011). It is built on three pillars: **story, slide design and delivery**. It also covers beginning, middle and end, key messages, template consistency, focal points, and talking *with* the audience instead of at it.
- **David JP Phillips, [*How to avoid death by PowerPoint*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Iwpi1Lm6dFo)** (TEDxStockholmSalon). His advice:
  - one message per slide;
  - long text goes into the notes, and the slide keeps short words and a visual anchor;
  - use size to show what matters most;
  - use contrast to steer attention;
  - cut the number of competing objects.

  Treat his specific numbers (e.g. "six objects", the retention percentages) as heuristics, not proven norms. Judge "dark background" advice by the actual room and contrast.

Other books share the title (Forsyth, Kerr, Flocker). Don't attribute their claims to the authors above.

## 1. Hierarchy: where to look first
Every slide has one job. Within 2 to 3 seconds the viewer should see the main element and what belongs to it. Show importance with **size, contrast, position and empty space**, not by making everything big and bright at once.

*Test:* shrink the slide to a thumbnail. If the logo, decorative background or slide number dominates, fix the hierarchy.

## 2. Composition and grouping
- Put captions next to the objects they describe. Separate groups with space or a subtle frame (Gestalt proximity).
- Align to a grid and leave margins. Empty space doesn't have to be white, and it isn't wasted.
- Repeat the style of recurring elements: headings, callouts, sources, actions. An accent works only when it is the exception.
- A **stage version** has less text and more visual pauses. A **document version** keeps the connections, captions and context so people can read it without you.

## 3. Reduce load, not meaning
- Remove decoration and convoluted wording.
- Split a complex process into steps and highlight the current one.
- Define a term before you use it.
- Sometimes keep things side by side, so the viewer doesn't have to remember the previous slide.

*Instead of* five paragraphs about AI adoption, use the headline "Start with one repetitive task", three steps "pick → test → roll out" and one example. Details go into the notes or a handout.

## 4. Headlines and text
- For explanatory slides, use an assertion headline: "Automation saves time on drafts", not "Automation". Only claim what you can show.
- Microsoft advises not going below **18 pt** and going larger in big rooms. That's a floor, not a guarantee: screen size, distance, typeface and contrast all matter.
- Use one or two type families that cover all your scripts (Latin + Cyrillic).

## 5. Colour and accessibility
- Make sure text has enough contrast with its background. Over photos, use a scrim, a gradient or a solid panel.
- Don't carry meaning by colour alone: add a label, a shape or a line style.
- For digital handouts and HTML decks, use **WCAG 2.2 AA** as a measurable target: **4.5:1** for normal text and **3:1** for large text. WCAG is a web standard, so check readability on the real projector too.
- In PPTX: give every slide a unique title, check the reading order, and add alt text to meaningful images. Run PowerPoint's Accessibility Checker.

## 6. Data, diagrams and images
- Pick the chart for the question: bars to compare categories, a line for change over time, a diagram for a process.
- Chart titles state the conclusion. Show units and the source with its date.
- Don't truncate axes in a way that misleads.
- Highlight the series that matters with an accent colour and keep the rest muted.
- Every image has a job: prove, give context, explain, or carry emotion. A random stock photo adds nothing.
- An AI-generated image is never photographic evidence. Label it if it could be mistaken for one.
- Keep diagrams and text editable where you can.

## 7. Per-slide checklist
- [ ] Clear job and main conclusion
- [ ] Obvious hierarchy, visible as a thumbnail
- [ ] Readable from the back row
- [ ] Captions sit next to their objects
- [ ] Sources visible where needed
- [ ] No overflow, bad crops or broken line breaks
- [ ] Still works in greyscale
- [ ] The translated version means the same and fits
