# 06 · Storytelling and humor

## Story: person, obstacle, change
Following Nancy Duarte, the **audience is the hero** and the speaker is the mentor. Move back and forth between **what is** and **what could be**, and end with a call to action.

1. Start the build with one sentence: *"After this talk the listener will be able to find an old fragment and turn it into material for a new talk."*
2. Pick a **specific episode** with an obstacle, a decision and a visible result. Example from the case study:
   > "Where is that slide?" → 1,141 files → an inventory → slow image captioning, so we switch to OCR → slide 57 in one deck matches slide 69 in another → three new talk formats.
3. Show the **evidence at the moment** the audience wants to see it.
4. Bio and tool descriptions get only as much time as the audience needs to understand the solution.
5. For each pair of slides, write separately what the viewer *sees*, what they *hear* and what *changes* between frames. Leave a question in the transition ("Where is the same fragment in the other version?") and answer it on the next slide.
6. End with a small first step: *"Open your longest talk. Ask AI for five key points with slide numbers and three versions for different audiences."*

## Humor: a joke should carry a point
- Tie the joke to a **pain the audience knows**, ideally your own experience. *"Everyone has `final_really.pptx`. Mine is `final_really_128slides_MEGA.pptx`."* It works because the next slide really shows 128 slides.
- Structure: **set-up → expectation → short twist → pause → bridge to the point.**
- If you deliver the punchline out loud, **don't put it on the slide** in advance.
- If a joke needs explaining, simplify it or drop it.
- In a screencast, leave time to see the image, read the caption and come back to your voice.
- A callback in the ending works well. Bring back the opening joke with a twist.
- Present personal numbers (what you pay, what you saved) as *your* experience, not as a promise.

## Memes and generated images
- Ask for **three different concepts**, not three wordings of the same caption.
- An image brief should include:
  - the reference photo;
  - expression and pose;
  - the object of the joke;
  - the composition and palette;
  - room for a caption.
- If the generator garbles text, generate the image **without text** and add the caption as an editable text box in the slide.
- For decks you share, use your own photos, original illustrations or properly licensed material. Check the source and licence of other people's memes.
- When a slide has several memes, guide the attention: say which one to look at, or enlarge one.

**Image prompt template**
> Use the attached portrait as a reference of the same person. Square photographic stand-up poster: almost black stage, one warm amber spotlight. The person looks into the camera with a deadpan face, pointing at [absurd prop that shows the pain, e.g. "a leaning tower of identical folders labelled FINAL_REALLY.pptx"]. Keep the face, age and clothing. Large character, readable silhouette, light film grain. No other text, logos or frames. Leave the bottom quarter empty for a caption.

## Test it on a person
Before the event, ask someone from the target audience:
- Does the joke land without explanation?
- Who is the butt of the joke?
- Does the story leave you knowing what to do next?
